If you've been told your BMI is "high" but you don't feel out of shape, you're not imagining the disconnect — Body Mass Index only looks at height and weight, while **Body Roundness Index (BRI)** adds waist circumference to estimate how your fat is distributed, not just how much you weigh for your height. Research published in *JAMA Network Open* (2024) found BRI tracks mortality risk in a clear pattern across a large US adult cohort, something BMI alone doesn't capture nearly as well for people with high muscle mass or fat concentrated around the waist. Neither number replaces a doctor, but if you want a quick second opinion beyond the scale, BRI fills a real gap that BMI leaves open.

## BRI vs. BMI at a Glance

| | BMI | BRI |
|---|---|---|
| Inputs needed | Height, weight | Height, waist circumference |
| What it estimates | Overall weight-for-height | Body shape / central fat distribution |
| Formula basis | Weight ÷ height² | Geometric model treating the body as an ellipse |
| Blind spot | Can't tell muscle from fat | Doesn't factor in weight at all |
| Typical "healthy" range | 18.5–24.9 | Roughly 3.41–5.46 (reference range, varies by cohort) |
| Best used for | Fast population-level screening | Estimating central (abdominal) fat risk |
| Can mislabel | Athletes, muscular people | People with unusual torso-to-leg proportions |

Neither metric diagnoses anything on its own — both are screening tools, and the people who benefit most from checking both are those whose BMI and visual body shape seem to disagree.

## What BRI Actually Measures That BMI Doesn't

BMI treats a 5'10", 180-pound bodybuilder and a 5'10", 180-pound person with a large waist and little muscle identically — same height, same weight, same BMI. But those two people likely carry very different amounts of visceral (abdominal) fat, which is more strongly linked to metabolic risk than total body weight. BRI, developed by mathematician Diana Thomas in 2013, models the body as an ellipse using height and waist circumference to estimate how "round" versus "elongated" someone's shape is — a proxy for central fat that BMI simply can't see. ## What the 2024 Research Actually Found

A 2024 cohort study in *JAMA Network Open* analyzed BRI against mortality risk and found a **U-shaped curve** — both unusually low and unusually high BRI were associated with increased risk, with the lowest risk clustering in a middle reference band (roughly BRI 4.45 to under 5.46 in that cohort). People in the highest BRI group had up to a 49% higher relative mortality risk than the reference group, while people in the lowest group also showed somewhat elevated risk — likely reflecting low muscle mass or underlying illness rather than "too healthy." This U-shape is the key difference from BMI, where the relationship between BMI and risk is also non-linear but far more familiar and far less waist-specific. It's worth repeating: these are cohort averages from one study population, not individual diagnostic cutoffs — your doctor, your family history, and your overall health picture matter more than where you fall on any single chart.

## Where BMI Still Wins

BMI isn't obsolete — it's just limited. It only requires a scale and a known height, making it faster for large-scale screening, and decades of population data make its categories familiar to clinicians everywhere. If you want to track weight trends over time, or you don't have a flexible tape measure handy, BMI is still a reasonable starting point. ## Where BRI Fills the Gap

BRI's advantage shows up specifically for people BMI tends to mislabel:

- **Muscular or athletic people** — a high BMI from muscle mass typically comes with a normal waist circumference, so BRI usually reads lower and more accurately than BMI in these cases.
- **People with a "skinny-fat" body composition** — a normal BMI can hide a disproportionately large waist, which BRI is specifically designed to catch.
- **Anyone tracking midsection changes** — because BRI is driven almost entirely by the waist-to-height ratio, it's more sensitive to abdominal fat loss or gain than BMI, which barely moves unless total weight changes substantially.

If your BMI and BRI results seem to disagree, that gap itself is useful information — it usually means your body composition (how much of your weight is muscle vs. fat, and where that fat sits) differs from what BMI alone would suggest. ## How to Measure Your Waist Correctly (It Changes the Result)

Because BRI is waist-driven, measurement technique matters more here than it does for BMI:

1. Measure at the **navel level**, not at the narrowest point of your waist or at your natural hip bone.
2. Stand up straight, breathe out normally, and measure — don't suck in your stomach or measure after a large meal.
3. Keep the tape snug but not compressing the skin.
4. Measure first thing in the morning for the most consistent day-to-day comparison if you're tracking changes over time.

A waist measurement taken 2 inches off in either direction can shift your BRI category, so consistency in how and when you measure matters more than getting a single "perfect" reading.

## Should You Use Both?

Yes — they're complementary, not competing. BMI gives you a fast, familiar weight-for-height baseline; BRI adds a shape-based signal that's more specific to abdominal fat and, per the 2024 cohort data, more directly tied to that U-shaped mortality pattern. Checking both takes under two minutes combined and gives you a fuller picture than either number alone — especially if you fall in a BMI category that feels like it doesn't match how you actually look or feel.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Is BRI more accurate than BMI?**
"More accurate" depends on what you're trying to estimate. For overall weight-for-height screening, BMI is simpler and well-studied. For estimating central/abdominal fat and its associated health risk, BRI's inclusion of waist circumference generally makes it more informative — but both are screening tools, not diagnostic ones.

**Can I have a normal BMI but a high BRI?**
Yes. This is common for people with a larger waist relative to their height but a weight that still falls in the "normal" BMI range — sometimes called "skinny-fat." It's one of the main reasons to check BRI alongside BMI rather than relying on BMI alone.

**Can I have a high BMI but a normal BRI?**
Yes, especially for muscular or athletic people. A high BMI driven by muscle mass rather than fat typically comes with a proportionate waist, so BRI often lands in or near the reference range even when BMI reads "overweight."

**What's a "good" BRI score?**
Based on the 2024 JAMA Network Open cohort data, the lowest mortality risk clustered in a middle reference band (roughly 4.45 to under 5.46), with risk increasing at both the low and high ends. These are population averages, not individual cutoffs — talk to a healthcare provider about what your number means for you specifically.

**Does BRI work differently for men and women?**
The BRI formula itself doesn't adjust for sex, but typical waist-to-height proportions differ between men and women, so reference ranges from cohort studies are usually reported with this in mind. Check the [BRI calculator](/calculators/body-roundness-index-calculator) FAQ for more detail on how this plays out in practice.

**Do I need any special equipment to calculate BRI?**
No — just a flexible tape measure for your waist and your height. The [BRI calculator](/calculators/body-roundness-index-calculator) handles the formula for you in both imperial and metric units.
