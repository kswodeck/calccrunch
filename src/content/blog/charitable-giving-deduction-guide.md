Starting with the 2026 tax year, charitable giving got a new tax benefit for most Americans — and a new restriction for others. Thanks to the One Big Beautiful Bill Act (OBBBA), if you take the **standard deduction** (about 90% of filers), you can now deduct up to **$1,000 ($2,000 if married filing jointly)** in cash donations directly on Form 1040, with no itemizing required. If you **itemize**, a new rule means your charitable deductions only count once they exceed **0.5% of your adjusted gross income (AGI)**. ## Two Different Rules, Depending on How You File

Before 2026, if you took the standard deduction, charitable gifts gave you zero additional tax benefit — the only way to deduct a donation was to itemize on Schedule A. OBBBA changed that by creating an entirely new, separate deduction for non-itemizers, while simultaneously tightening the rules for itemizers. Here's the side-by-side:

| | Standard Deduction (new) | Itemized Deduction (changed) |
|---|---|---|
| Who it's for | ~90% of filers | Filers with Schedule A deductions exceeding the standard deduction |
| Deduction cap | $1,000 single/HOH/MFS, $2,000 MFJ | No dollar cap (subject to AGI % limits) |
| New 0.5% AGI floor? | No — doesn't apply | Yes, starting 2026 |
| Eligible gifts | Cash only, to public charities | Cash and non-cash property |
| Donor-advised funds? | Not eligible | Eligible (subject to normal limits) |
| Unused amount carries forward? | No | Yes, up to 5 years |

Because the two rules are structured so differently, the right move depends entirely on which one applies to you — which is exactly what the [charitable giving deduction calculator](/calculators/charitable-giving-deduction-calculator) is built to sort out.

## The New Non-Itemizer Deduction, Explained

If you take the standard deduction, you can now claim an "above-the-line" deduction for cash gifts to qualifying 501(c)(3) public charities, up to:

- **$1,000** for single, head of household, or married filing separately filers
- **$2,000** for married couples filing jointly

A few important details:

- **Cash only.** Checks, debit/credit cards, ACH transfers, payroll deductions, and apps like Venmo, PayPal, or Apple Pay all count. Donated clothing, household goods, or appreciated stock do not qualify for this particular deduction.
- **Public charities only.** Gifts to donor-advised funds (DAFs) or private non-operating foundations don't count toward this cap, even though they're cash gifts.
- **Permanent, but not inflation-indexed.** Unlike many tax figures, this $1,000/$2,000 cap won't automatically rise with inflation in future years — it stays fixed unless Congress changes it again.
- **No carryforward.** If you give $1,500 in cash as a single filer, $500 of that simply isn't deductible this year or any future year. There's no mechanism to carry the excess forward.
- **No Schedule A needed.** This deduction is claimed directly on Form 1040, after your AGI is calculated — you don't need to itemize anything to get it.

For example, a married couple filing jointly who donates $2,000 in cash to their church gets a deduction worth several hundred dollars in tax savings that, before 2026, would have been worth nothing if they used the standard deduction. ## The New 0.5% AGI Floor for Itemizers

If you itemize deductions on Schedule A, OBBBA adds a new limitation: your charitable contributions are only deductible to the extent they exceed **0.5% of your AGI**. This floor is permanent and took effect January 1, 2026.

Here's how the floor plays out at different income levels, assuming $5,000 in annual giving:

| AGI | 0.5% Floor | Total Giving | Deductible Amount |
|---|---|---|---|
| $50,000 | $250 | $5,000 | $4,750 |
| $100,000 | $500 | $5,000 | $4,500 |
| $200,000 | $1,000 | $5,000 | $4,000 |
| $400,000 | $2,000 | $5,000 | $3,000 |

Notice that the floor doesn't eliminate your deduction — it just shaves a small amount off the top before the rest counts. But if your total annual giving is modest relative to your income, the floor can wipe out your deduction entirely. A filer with $300,000 AGI who donates $1,000 in a single year gets a floor of $1,500 — meaning that gift generates **zero** deduction.

One widely-used workaround is **"bunching"**: instead of giving $1,000 every year, consider giving $3,000-$5,000 every two or three years. You'll clear the 0.5% floor in the year you bunch your giving, and you can take the standard deduction (or the new non-itemizer cash deduction) in the off years.

It's also worth noting that OBBBA separately made permanent the 60%-of-AGI ceiling on cash gifts to public charities (which was otherwise set to revert to 50% after 2025), and the 30%-of-AGI ceiling generally applies to appreciated property. Most households never come close to these ceilings, but high-income donors making large gifts should keep them in mind — amounts above the ceiling carry forward up to five years.

## Should You Itemize or Take the Standard Deduction?

This calculator assumes you already know which path applies to you, but it's worth double-checking before you file. Add up your likely Schedule A deductions — mortgage interest, state and local taxes (capped at $10,000-$40,000 depending on income under OBBBA's SALT changes), medical expenses above 7.5% of AGI, and charitable gifts after the 0.5% floor. If that total is **less** than your standard deduction, you're better off taking the standard deduction — and claiming the new $1,000/$2,000 cash-giving benefit on top of it. If it's **more**, itemizing wins, and your charitable gifts are deductible subject to the new floor. ## Year-End Moves to Make Before December 31

1. **Confirm your gifts are cash, not property, if you're relying on the non-itemizer deduction.** Only cash counts toward the $1,000/$2,000 cap.
2. **Get your documentation in order.** You need a bank record or written acknowledgment from the charity for any gift, and a contemporaneous written acknowledgment for any single donation of $250 or more.
3. **Consider bunching if you itemize and your giving is close to the 0.5% floor.** Front-load two or three years of planned giving into 2026 (or wait and bunch into 2027) to clear the floor in the year you give.
4. **Time large gifts around Giving Tuesday (December 1, 2026) or year-end**, but make sure the transaction actually processes — and the charity receives it — by December 31 to count for the 2026 tax year.
5. **Update your budget** for any lump-sum giving using the [budget calculator](/calculators/budget-calculator) so a bunched donation doesn't catch your cash flow off guard.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Do I need to itemize to deduct charitable donations in 2026?**
No. Starting in 2026, you can deduct up to $1,000 ($2,000 if married filing jointly) in cash donations to public charities even if you take the standard deduction. This is a brand-new benefit under the One Big Beautiful Bill Act.

**What counts as a "public charity" for the non-itemizer deduction?**
A 501(c)(3) organization classified as a public charity — think churches, schools, food banks, and most well-known nonprofits. Donor-advised funds and private non-operating foundations are specifically excluded.

**How does the 0.5% AGI floor affect itemizers?**
If you itemize, you subtract 0.5% of your AGI from your total charitable contributions before the remainder is deductible. On $150,000 AGI, that's a $750 floor — only giving above $750 counts.

**Can I combine both deductions in the same year?**
No. You either take the standard deduction (and can use the new $1,000/$2,000 cash deduction) or you itemize (and your gifts are subject to the 0.5% floor). You can't use both benefits on the same return.

**Is this a temporary tax break that will expire?**
No. Both the non-itemizer deduction and the 0.5% AGI floor for itemizers are written into the tax code as permanent provisions, not temporary measures set to sunset like many TCJA-era rules.

## The Bottom Line

For the roughly 90% of taxpayers who take the standard deduction, 2026 brings a genuinely new tax benefit: up to $1,000 ($2,000 married filing jointly) in cash giving is now deductible with no itemizing required. For itemizers, the picture is more mixed — a new 0.5% AGI floor means smaller annual gifts may no longer generate any deduction at all, making strategies like bunching more valuable than ever.
