Tax-loss harvesting means selling an investment that's worth less than you paid for it — on purpose, before December 31 — so the loss can offset capital gains elsewhere in your portfolio and lower your tax bill. Losses first cancel out gains dollar-for-dollar; any leftover loss can then deduct up to **$3,000** of ordinary income ($1,500 if you're married filing separately), and whatever's still left over **carries forward indefinitely** to future tax years. With the year-end deadline approaching, here's exactly how it works and how to estimate what it's actually worth to you.

## The December 31 Deadline

To count for the 2026 tax year, the **trade date** — not the settlement date — has to fall on or before December 31, 2026. A sale executed on December 31 still counts even if it settles a day or two later, in early January. Once the calendar flips to a new year, any losses you didn't realize are gone for that tax year; you can't go back and harvest 2026 losses in 2027.

This is also why late November through December is the busiest window for this strategy — it's the last chance to review your portfolio for underwater positions before the window closes.

## How the Loss Gets Applied

The IRS nets capital gains and losses in a specific order, and understanding it matters because where your loss lands determines how much it actually saves you:

1. **Short-term losses offset short-term gains first.** Short-term gains are taxed as ordinary income (up to 37%), so eliminating them generally saves you the most per dollar of loss.
2. **Long-term losses offset long-term gains next.** Long-term gains are taxed at the lower 0%/15%/20% rates, so the tax saved per dollar here is smaller — unless you're in a high bracket, where the savings can still be meaningful.
3. **Any loss still left over can offset up to $3,000 of ordinary income** ($1,500 if married filing separately) — wages, self-employment income, interest, anything.
4. **Whatever's left after that carries forward** to next year, and the year after, indefinitely, until it's fully used.

**Example**: You've realized $2,000 of short-term gains and $10,000 of long-term gains this year. You harvest a $15,000 loss on an underwater tech stock. The loss first wipes out the $2,000 short-term gain, then the $10,000 long-term gain, leaving $3,000 — which offsets $3,000 of your ordinary income. Nothing carries forward. | Where the loss goes | Amount | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Short-term gains | $2,000 | Offsets ordinary-rate gains first (biggest savings per dollar) |
| Long-term gains | $10,000 | Offsets preferential-rate gains next |
| Ordinary income | $3,000 | Annual cap ($1,500 if MFS) |
| Carryforward to 2027 | $0 | Fully used this year |

## How Much Can It Actually Save You?

Your savings depend on your marginal tax rate and exactly what the loss offsets — a loss that cancels a short-term gain saves you your ordinary rate (10%-37%) on that portion, while a loss that cancels a long-term gain saves you the long-term rate (0%, 15%, or 20%) that would have applied instead. ## The Wash-Sale Rule — The Part Everyone Trips On

The IRS won't let you harvest a loss and then immediately buy the same (or a "substantially identical") investment back, because that defeats the purpose of actually changing your position. The wash-sale rule (IRC Section 1091) disallows your loss if you buy a substantially identical security within **30 days before or after** the sale — a **61-day window** in total.

<div class="callout">
<strong>The loss isn't gone forever if you trigger a wash sale</strong> — it's added to the cost basis of your replacement shares, which reduces your future gain (or increases a future loss) when you eventually sell those shares. You just don't get the tax benefit <em>this year</em>, which is the whole point of harvesting before the deadline.
</div>

A few practical ways people accidentally trigger a wash sale without realizing it:

- **Automatic dividend reinvestment (DRIP).** If DRIP is turned on for the position you're selling, a routine dividend purchase within the 30-day window can taint the entire loss. Turn off DRIP before harvesting.
- **Buying in a different account.** The rule applies across **all your accounts** — including a spouse's accounts and IRAs/401(k)s — not just the one where you sold.
- **Buying back too early in a different month.** If you harvest in December, remember to look back 30 days *before* the sale too — a purchase in late November can retroactively create a wash sale on a December sale.

Crypto held directly is currently an exception: the wash-sale rule only applies to "stock or securities," and the IRS treats cryptocurrency as property, so you can sell crypto at a loss and immediately buy it back without triggering a wash sale under current law.

## A Simple Harvesting Checklist

1. **List your underwater positions** — anything currently worth less than you paid for it.
2. **Check what gains you've already realized this year**, short-term and long-term separately.
3. **Decide how much loss to harvest.** You don't need to sell an entire position — partial sales work too.
4. **Run the numbers** with the [tax-loss harvesting calculator](/calculators/tax-loss-harvesting-calculator) to see your estimated savings and how much (if any) carries forward.
5. **If you plan to rebuy, wait at least 31 days**, or buy something similar but not "substantially identical" (a different fund tracking a different index, for example) to stay invested without triggering the wash sale.
6. **Execute the trade by December 31** — trade date, not settlement date.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Is tax-loss harvesting worth it for small losses?**
It can still help — losses offset gains dollar-for-dollar with no minimum, and even a modest loss can chip away at the $3,000 ordinary-income deduction if you have no gains to offset. The main thing to weigh is whether transaction costs or giving up a position you still believe in outweighs the tax benefit.

**Can I harvest losses in a 401(k) or IRA?**
No — losses inside tax-advantaged retirement accounts aren't deductible because those accounts aren't taxed on gains or losses in the first place. Tax-loss harvesting only applies to taxable brokerage accounts.

**What happens to a loss that carries forward if I don't have gains next year?**
It can still offset up to $3,000 ($1,500 MFS) of ordinary income that year, and continues carrying forward indefinitely — there's no expiration — until it's fully used, even if that takes many years.

**Does tax-loss harvesting change my investment strategy long-term?**
It shouldn't, if done correctly. The goal is to capture a tax benefit while staying invested in a similar asset class (avoiding the wash sale), not to abandon your overall allocation.

**Do I need to report harvested losses even if they don't fully offset gains this year?**
Yes — report all sales on Schedule D / Form 8949 regardless of whether the loss is fully used, offsets income, or carries forward; the carryforward amount itself gets tracked on next year's return.

## The Bottom Line

Tax-loss harvesting is one of the few tax strategies that's entirely within your control and has a hard, predictable deadline — December 31. The mechanics are straightforward once you see the netting order (gains first, then up to $3,000 of ordinary income, then carryforward), but the wash-sale rule is where most people accidentally lose the benefit. Before you sell anything, run your numbers through the [tax-loss harvesting calculator](/calculators/tax-loss-harvesting-calculator) to see exactly what you'd save — and double check your bracket with the [tax bracket calculator](/calculators/tax-bracket-calculator) if you're not sure where you land.
