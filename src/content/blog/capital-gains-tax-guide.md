Capital gains tax is the federal tax you owe on the profit from selling an asset — stock, crypto, a rental property — for more than you paid for it. How much you owe comes down to one key question: did you hold the asset for **more than one year**? If yes, your profit qualifies for the lower long-term rates of 0%, 15%, or 20%. If no, it's taxed as ordinary income at your regular tax bracket, up to 37%. High earners may also owe an extra 3.8% surtax on top of either rate. Here's exactly how each piece works, with a worked example.

## Short-Term vs. Long-Term: The One-Year Line

The entire capital gains system hinges on your **holding period** — the time between when you acquired an asset and when you sold it.

- **Short-term capital gains**: the asset was held **one year or less**. These gains are added to your other income and taxed at your regular marginal tax rate (the same brackets used for wages) — anywhere from 10% to 37% for 2026.
- **Long-term capital gains**: the asset was held **more than one year**. These gains get preferential treatment — taxed at 0%, 15%, or 20% depending on your total taxable income, almost always lower than your ordinary rate.

This is why "just wait a few more weeks" is common advice near the one-year mark on an investment that's up — crossing from short-term to long-term status can cut your tax rate roughly in half or more, depending on your bracket. You can check your current ordinary bracket with the [tax bracket calculator](/calculators/tax-bracket-calculator) to see exactly how big that gap is for your income level.

## 2026 Long-Term Capital Gains Tax Brackets

Long-term rates are based on your **total taxable income** — your ordinary income plus the gain itself — not a flat percentage of the gain alone. Here are the official 2026 thresholds (IRS Revenue Procedure 2025-32):

| Filing Status | 0% Rate (up to) | 15% Rate (up to) | 20% Rate (above) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Single | $49,450 | $545,500 | $545,500 |
| Married Filing Jointly | $98,900 | $613,700 | $613,700 |
| Head of Household | $66,200 | $579,600 | $579,600 |
| Married Filing Separately | $49,450 | $306,850 | $306,850 |

The key detail most people miss: your long-term gain **stacks on top of** your other taxable income, not the other way around. If your ordinary income already fills up the 0% bracket, your gain starts getting taxed at 15% from the first dollar — it doesn't get its own fresh 0% allowance.

**Example**: A single filer with $40,000 of other taxable income sells stock for a $20,000 long-term gain. The first $9,450 of that gain (filling the gap up to the $49,450 threshold) is taxed at 0%. The remaining $10,550 falls above the threshold and is taxed at 15% — about $1,583 in federal tax on a $20,000 gain, an effective rate of roughly 7.9%. ## Short-Term Gains: Taxed Like a Bonus

Short-term gains don't get their own bracket table — they're simply added to your other income and taxed at whatever marginal rate that pushes you into. If you're already in the 22% bracket and a short-term trade adds $10,000 of taxable income, that $10,000 is taxed at 22% (or partially at 22% and partially at the next bracket up, if it pushes you across a threshold).

This is the main reason short-term trading is tax-inefficient compared to long-term investing for the exact same dollar amount of profit — the rate gap between a 22%-37% ordinary bracket and a 0%-20% long-term rate can be substantial.

## The 3.8% Net Investment Income Tax (NIIT)

On top of either the ordinary rate or the long-term rate, higher earners owe an additional **3.8% Net Investment Income Tax** on investment income — including capital gains — once their modified adjusted gross income (MAGI) crosses a threshold:

| Filing Status | 2026 NIIT Threshold |
|---|---|
| Single / Head of Household | $200,000 |
| Married Filing Jointly | $250,000 |
| Married Filing Separately | $125,000 |

These thresholds are **not** adjusted for inflation and have stayed fixed since the tax took effect in 2013, which means more taxpayers cross them every year as incomes rise. The NIIT applies to the *smaller* of your net investment income or the amount your MAGI exceeds the threshold — not your entire gain automatically. The [capital gains tax calculator](/calculators/capital-gains-tax-calculator) checks this threshold against your inputs and flags exactly how much of your gain is subject to the extra 3.8%.

## How to Estimate Your Capital Gains Tax Bill

1. **Figure out your holding period.** More than one year = long-term; one year or less = short-term.
2. **Know your other taxable income** for the year — wages, self-employment income, interest, etc., after deductions. The [tax bracket calculator](/calculators/tax-bracket-calculator) can help you land on this number if you haven't calculated it yet.
3. **Enter your gain amount and holding period** into the [capital gains tax calculator](/calculators/capital-gains-tax-calculator) along with your filing status and other income.
4. **Review the breakdown** — it shows exactly how much of your gain falls into each rate band, whether the NIIT applies, and your total estimated federal tax and after-tax proceeds.

## A Few Ways to Legally Reduce What You Owe

- **Hold past the one-year mark.** If you're close to the line on a profitable position, waiting a few extra weeks to qualify for long-term rates is often worth more than any short-term trading edge you'd otherwise capture.
- **Offset gains with losses.** Selling a losing position in the same year nets against your gains dollar-for-dollar, and up to $3,000 of net losses beyond that can offset ordinary income too, with the excess carried forward to future years.
- **Donate appreciated stock instead of cash.** Giving long-held, appreciated shares directly to a charity lets you potentially avoid the capital gains tax on the appreciation entirely while still claiming a deduction for the fair market value if you itemize. See the [charitable giving deduction calculator](/calculators/charitable-giving-deduction-calculator) to estimate the deduction side of that strategy.
- **Time large gains across tax years.** If a sale isn't urgent, splitting it across two calendar years can sometimes keep more of it inside a lower bracket instead of pushing it all into a higher one in a single year.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**What's the difference between short-term and long-term capital gains?**
Short-term gains come from assets held one year or less and are taxed as ordinary income (10%-37%). Long-term gains come from assets held more than one year and get preferential 0%/15%/20% federal rates.

**Do I owe capital gains tax on crypto the same way as stocks?**
Yes. The IRS treats cryptocurrency as property, so the same short-term vs. long-term holding-period rules and tax rates apply to crypto gains as to stock or other investment gains.

**What counts as my "cost basis" for figuring out the gain?**
Generally what you originally paid for the asset, plus certain costs like broker commissions or improvements (for real estate). Sale price minus cost basis equals your taxable gain.

**Is capital gains tax the same in every state?**
No — this guide covers federal tax only. Most states that have an income tax also tax capital gains, usually as ordinary income at the state's regular rates, on top of the federal amount.

**Can my capital gains tax rate ever be 0%?**
Yes. If your total taxable income (including the gain) stays under the 0% threshold for your filing status — $49,450 for single filers in 2026, for example — your long-term capital gains are taxed at 0% federally.

## The Bottom Line

Capital gains tax isn't one flat rate — it depends on how long you held the asset, how much other income you have, and whether you cross the NIIT threshold. Long-term gains stacked on moderate income can be taxed as low as 0%, while short-term gains on top of a high income can approach 37% plus the 3.8% surtax.
