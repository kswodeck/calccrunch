The IRS has officially set the 2027 Health FSA contribution limit at **$3,500**, up $100 from the 2026 limit of $3,400, under Revenue Procedure 2026-35 (announced November 5, 2026). The carryover limit for unused 2027 funds into 2028 also rises to **$700**, up from $680. If you're going through open enrollment right now, this is the number to use when deciding how much to set aside for next year — but your employer's plan won't bump your election automatically, so you still need to act.

## What Changed for 2027

| | 2026 | 2027 | Change |
|---|---|---|---|
| Health FSA contribution limit | $3,400 | $3,500 | +$100 |
| Health FSA carryover limit | $680 | $700 | +$20 |
| Dependent Care FSA limit | $5,000 (household) | $5,000 (household) | No change (set by statute, not indexed) |
| Limited-Purpose FSA limit | $3,400 | $3,500 | +$100 (tracks Health FSA) |

The Health FSA limit is indexed for inflation each year under IRC Section 125, which is why it moves annually. The Dependent Care FSA limit, by contrast, is fixed by statute at $5,000 per household ($2,500 if married filing separately) and hasn't changed since 1986 — Congress would have to pass new legislation to raise it, not the IRS.

## Why This Matters at Open Enrollment

FSA elections don't carry over or auto-adjust between plan years — they're a "use it or elect it again" benefit. Unlike a 401(k), there's no default percentage that quietly increases. If you don't actively update your election during open enrollment, many employers simply default you to **$0** or carry forward your prior dollar amount, missing out on the extra room entirely.

This matters most if you're already maxing out your FSA for recurring expenses — orthodontia, contacts, prescriptions, therapy copays — since the full $3,500 lets you set aside $100 more pretax before you ever see it as income. At a 22% federal bracket plus 7.65% payroll tax, that extra $100 of pretax room is worth roughly $30 in avoided tax if you have the eligible expenses to use it. ## How Much Should You Actually Elect?

The biggest FSA risk is the "use it or lose it" rule — unused funds beyond the $700 carryover are forfeited at year-end (unless your plan offers a grace period instead, which some employers use in place of carryover). Before bumping your election up to the new $3,500 max, it helps to estimate your actual eligible expenses for the year:

1. **Add up predictable recurring costs** — prescription refills, contact lenses or glasses, therapy or counseling copays, orthodontia payments, planned dental work.
2. **Include expenses you can predict with reasonable confidence** — a planned surgery, a new baby's first-year pediatric copays, PT sessions already scheduled.
3. **Leave out speculative expenses** — "might need new glasses" doesn't belong in your FSA math the same way a signed treatment plan does.
4. **Compare your total against $3,500 and the $700 carryover cushion.** If your predictable expenses land under $2,800, the carryover alone covers the gap between what you use and what's left — electing the full max becomes much lower-risk.

If you're also saving toward a broader financial goal — an emergency fund, a planned purchase — the [savings goal calculator](/calculators/savings-goal-calculator) can help you see how redirecting payroll dollars into an FSA versus a savings account changes your timeline, since FSA dollars are pretax but restricted to qualified medical expenses.

## FSA vs. HSA: Don't Mix These Up

A Health FSA is not the same as a Health Savings Account (HSA), and the two have very different rules:

- **A Health FSA** is use-it-or-lose-it (with limited carryover/grace period), employer-owned, and available regardless of what health plan you're on — though a *Limited-Purpose* FSA is required if you're also contributing to an HSA.
- **An HSA** has no annual forfeiture, is fully portable if you change jobs, and requires an HSA-eligible high-deductible health plan. HSA limits for 2027 are announced separately (typically alongside other HSA figures) and follow a different IRS revenue procedure than the FSA limit covered here.

If your employer offers both, check which one your health plan actually qualifies you for before setting your election — you generally can't max out a general-purpose Health FSA and an HSA in the same year under the same plan.

## Dependent Care FSA: No Change for 2027

The Dependent Care FSA limit stays at $5,000 per household for 2027 ($2,500 if married filing separately) — this is a statutory cap under IRC Section 129, not adjusted by the same inflation formula as the Health FSA, so it doesn't move most years. If you're budgeting for childcare, after-school care, or adult dependent care costs, plan around this unchanged figure rather than expecting an increase. ## A Quick Open Enrollment Checklist

1. **Confirm your employer has adopted the new 2027 limits** — plans aren't required to allow the full IRS maximum, so check your enrollment portal or benefits summary.
2. **Estimate your predictable medical expenses** for 2027 using the steps above.
3. **Decide your Health FSA election** — up to $3,500, factoring in the $700 carryover cushion if your plan offers one.
4. **Set your Dependent Care FSA election separately** — still capped at $5,000 per household.
5. **Submit your election during your open enrollment window** — missing the deadline usually means $0 for a Health FSA (no automatic continuation) unless your employer defaults differently.
6. **Check whether unused 2026 carryover funds are set to expire** — the carryover itself has a deadline, typically tied to when your new plan year's funds become accessible.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**What is the 2027 FSA contribution limit?**
The IRS set the 2027 Health FSA limit at $3,500 per employee, up $100 from the 2026 limit of $3,400, per Revenue Procedure 2026-35.

**Did the FSA carryover limit also increase for 2027?**
Yes — the maximum carryover from the 2027 plan year into 2028 rises to $700, up from $680 in 2026. Not all employers offer a carryover provision, so check your specific plan.

**Is the Dependent Care FSA limit changing for 2027?**
No. The Dependent Care FSA limit remains $5,000 per household ($2,500 if married filing separately) for 2027. This limit is set by statute, not indexed for inflation, so it only changes when Congress passes new legislation.

**Does my FSA election automatically increase to the new limit?**
No. You must actively elect your 2027 FSA amount during open enrollment. Employers do not automatically raise your contribution to match the new IRS maximum.

**What happens to unused FSA money at the end of the year?**
It depends on your employer's plan. Some offer a carryover (up to $700 for 2027), some offer a grace period of extra time to spend it, and some offer neither — in which case unused funds above any allowance are forfeited under the "use it or lose it" rule.

## The Bottom Line

The 2027 Health FSA limit of $3,500 gives you $100 more in pretax room than 2026, plus a $700 carryover cushion if your plan allows it — but none of that happens automatically. You have to elect it during open enrollment, and it's worth sizing your election against your real expected medical costs rather than just defaulting to the max.
