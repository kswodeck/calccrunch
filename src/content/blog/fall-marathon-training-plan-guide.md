## When Should You Start Training for a Fall Marathon?

For a typical fall marathon (October or November race day), start a standard training plan **16 weeks before race day**. That puts most training blocks kicking off in June or July, depending on the exact race date. If you can already run comfortably for about an hour without stopping, 16 weeks is enough time to build your long runs up to 18–20 miles, work in speed sessions, and taper properly before race day. Count backward from your specific race date using the table below, then use the [pace calculator](/calculators/pace-calculator) to set realistic training and goal paces as your plan progresses.

## Find Your Start Date: Countdown Table

Rather than guessing, count backward from your race date. Here's when to start for the three most common plan lengths:

| Plan Length | Best For | Race in Late September | Race in Mid-October | Race in Mid-November |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 12 weeks | Experienced runners already running 20+ miles/week | Late June | Late July | Late August |
| 16 weeks | Most runners with an existing running base | Early June | Early July | Early August |
| 20 weeks | Beginners or those returning after time off | Early May | Early June | Early July |

If today's date has already passed your ideal 16-week start, don't panic — a compressed 10–12 week plan can still get you to the starting line safely as long as you already have some running base. What matters more than hitting an exact week count is building mileage gradually and never jumping your weekly distance by more than about 10% at a time.

## Why 16 Weeks Is the Standard

Sixteen weeks sits in the middle of the evidence-supported range (roughly 14–20 weeks) for marathon preparation, and it's popular for a practical reason: it's long enough to build both cardiovascular fitness (which adapts relatively quickly) and the connective-tissue durability in tendons and joints (which adapts much more slowly), while still being short enough that most runners stay motivated through the whole cycle without burning out.

## What a 16-Week Plan Actually Looks Like

Every reputable marathon plan follows the same basic shape, regardless of pace:

1. **Weeks 1–4 (Base building):** Rebuild your weekly mileage and running habit. Mostly easy-paced runs, one weekly "long run" starting around 8–10 miles.
2. **Weeks 5–10 (Build phase):** Long runs grow by roughly 1 mile per week, with a step-back (shorter) week every third or fourth week to let your body absorb the training. Introduce tempo runs or intervals once a week.
3. **Weeks 11–14 (Peak phase):** Your longest runs (18–20 miles) happen here, along with your highest total weekly mileage. This is also when most runners run a tune-up half marathon or 20K race to test race-day pacing.
4. **Weeks 15–16 (Taper):** Mileage drops by 20–40% each week so your legs are fully recovered on race day, even though it can feel counterintuitive to run less right before the race.

## Setting Your Training Paces

Most marathon plans reference three or four distinct paces — easy, marathon goal pace, tempo, and interval pace — and getting them right matters more than hitting an exact mileage number. Enter a recent race result (a 5K or 10K time) into the [pace calculator](/calculators/pace-calculator) to get a predicted marathon finish time, then work backward to set your easy-run pace (typically 60–90 seconds per mile slower than goal marathon pace) and tempo pace (roughly 25–30 seconds per mile faster than goal pace).

## Common Fall Marathon Training Mistakes

- **Starting too late.** A rushed 6–8 week plan dramatically increases injury risk because connective tissue doesn't have time to adapt, even if your cardiovascular fitness improves quickly.
- **Skipping step-back weeks.** Continuously increasing mileage without a recovery week is one of the most common causes of overuse injuries like shin splints and IT band syndrome.
- **Ignoring summer heat during base building.** Since 16-week plans for fall races start in June or July, adjust effort (not just pace) for heat and humidity — running "by feel" on hot days prevents overtraining.
- **Skipping the taper.** Runners often feel great two weeks out and want to keep mileage high; trust the taper, since undertrained legs recover faster than overtrained ones.
- **Never practicing race-day nutrition.** Test your fueling plan (gels, sports drink, timing) on long runs well before race day — race morning is the wrong time to try something new.

## Cross-Training and Recovery

Running isn't the only thing that determines whether you make it to the start line healthy. Two habits separate runners who finish training strong from those who get sidelined by injury:

- **1–2 cross-training days per week.** Cycling, swimming, or the elliptical build aerobic fitness without the repetitive-impact stress of running, which is especially useful during the build and peak phases when total training load is highest.
- **Strength training twice a week.** Basic lower-body and core strength work (squats, lunges, planks, single-leg exercises) measurably reduces common marathon injuries like runner's knee and IT band syndrome, according to the sports-medicine literature on distance runners.

Sleep and rest days matter just as much as the runs themselves — this is when the adaptations from training (stronger muscles, denser bone, more efficient cardiovascular system) actually happen. Treat scheduled rest days as part of the plan, not an optional extra.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Is 16 weeks enough time to train for a marathon from scratch?**
If you can't yet run continuously for about an hour, 16 weeks is tight. A 20-week plan (or completing a "couch to 10K" progression first) is safer for true beginners.

**Can I train for a marathon with only 12 weeks?**
Yes, if you're already running consistently (20+ miles per week) with a recent long run of 8-plus miles. Runners starting from a lower base should stick to 16–20 weeks to reduce injury risk.

**How many days per week should I run during marathon training?**
Most 16-week plans use 4–5 running days per week, with 1–2 rest or cross-training days. Beginners often do well with 4 days; more experienced runners may run 5–6.

**Do I need to run the full 26.2 miles before race day?**
No. Most plans peak at 18–20 miles for the longest training run — your taper and race-day adrenaline carry you the rest of the way.

**What pace should my long runs be?**
Long runs are typically run at "easy" pace — noticeably slower than your marathon goal pace, often by 60–90 seconds per mile. The goal is time on your feet and aerobic endurance, not speed.
