December 31, 2026 is the hard deadline for most moves that can still shrink your 2026 tax bill — once the calendar flips to January, your options are mostly limited to IRA contributions. The five moves worth prioritizing before then: harvest investment losses (watch the 30-day wash sale rule), max out any remaining 401(k) payroll contributions, lock in your charitable giving, spend down "use it or lose it" FSA dollars, and double-check your withholding against your actual bracket. Here's exactly how each one works and the deadline you can't miss.

## Move 1: Harvest Your Investment Losses — Carefully

Tax-loss harvesting means selling an investment that's down to realize a loss you can use to offset gains elsewhere in your portfolio — or, if you have no gains, up to $3,000 a year against your ordinary income ($1,500 if married filing separately). Any loss beyond that carries forward indefinitely to future tax years.

The deadline is the **trade date**, not the settlement date — a sale executed on December 31 counts for 2026 even though the cash doesn't settle until January. Just remember the stock market closes early on Christmas Eve (December 24) and is closed on Christmas Day, so don't wait until the literal last moment.

The catch is the **wash sale rule**: if you sell a security at a loss and buy back the same or a "substantially identical" security within 30 days before or after the sale, the loss is disallowed and added to the cost basis of the new shares instead. That creates a 61-day danger zone around any sale. A common rule of thumb: if you plan to repurchase the same security, sell by **December 1** — that gives you a 30-day buffer so your repurchase lands safely in the new year without the window spilling backward into a position you already held.

If you want to stay invested in the market without the wash-sale risk, swap into something similar but not "substantially identical" — for example, selling a broad S&P 500 index fund and buying a total-market fund instead, rather than buying back the exact same ticker.

One 2026-specific wrinkle: cryptocurrency held directly is still treated as property, not a security, so the wash sale rule technically doesn't apply to crypto trades today. A bill that would extend wash-sale rules to crypto cleared a House committee in September 2026 but is not law as of this writing — worth watching if you're harvesting crypto losses this year.

## Move 2: Max Out Your 401(k) Before Your Last Paycheck

Unlike an IRA, which you can fund up until the April tax deadline, 401(k) contributions have to come out of your paycheck before the calendar year ends — there's no way to "catch up" in January for the prior year. The 2026 employee deferral limit is **$24,500**, with an additional **$11,250** "super catch-up" available if you're turning 60, 61, 62, or 63 during the year.

If you've been under-contributing, check your last one or two pay stubs of the year and see whether you can bump your contribution percentage enough to capture any remaining room before your final paycheck processes. ## Move 3: Lock In Your Charitable Giving

2026 brought real changes to how charitable deductions work, regardless of how you file. If you take the standard deduction, you can now deduct up to $1,000 ($2,000 married filing jointly) in cash gifts to public charities with no itemizing required — a brand-new benefit. If you itemize, your contributions are only deductible once they exceed a new floor equal to 0.5% of your AGI.

Either way, the gift has to actually be **processed and received by the charity by December 31** to count for the 2026 tax year — don't wait until the last few days if you're mailing a check or need the charity to confirm receipt. Run your specific numbers through the [charitable giving deduction calculator](/calculators/charitable-giving-deduction-calculator) to see exactly how much of your giving is deductible and what it's worth in tax savings — the rules work very differently depending on whether you itemize.

## Move 4: Spend Down Your FSA Before It Resets

If you have a Healthcare Flexible Spending Account (FSA), most plans operate on a strict "use it or lose it" basis — unused funds at year-end are typically forfeited unless your employer's specific plan offers a carryover or a grace period into the following year. Check with your HR or benefits team now, not in late December, so you have time to actually schedule appointments, refill prescriptions, or buy eligible items (contact lenses, orthotics, first-aid supplies) before your balance disappears.

## Move 5: Double-Check Your Withholding and Bracket

Before your final paycheck of the year, it's worth a quick sanity check: is your withholding tracking toward a refund that's way bigger (or smaller) than you expected? Life changes — a raise, a new job, a big investment gain or loss, buying a home — can all shift which bracket you land in and whether you're on pace to owe money or get a windfall refund. Use the [tax bracket calculator](/calculators/tax-bracket-calculator) to see your 2026 marginal and effective rates based on your actual income, and adjust your W-4 if the numbers are off. ## Frequently Asked Questions

**What's the actual deadline for tax-loss harvesting in 2026?**
The trade date needs to be on or before December 31, 2026. Settlement (when the cash actually moves) can happen in January and it still counts for the 2026 tax year — but the trade itself has to execute by year-end, and markets close early on Christmas Eve.

**How much can I deduct in capital losses if I don't have any gains to offset?**
Up to $3,000 per year against ordinary income ($1,500 if married filing separately). Any loss beyond that carries forward to future tax years indefinitely — it isn't lost.

**If I sell a stock at a loss and buy it back in January, do I still get the deduction?**
It depends on timing. If you repurchase the same or a substantially identical security within 30 days of the sale (before or after), the wash sale rule disallows the loss and adds it to your new cost basis instead. Selling by early December gives you enough of a buffer that a January repurchase falls outside the 30-day window.

**Can I still contribute to my 401(k) after December 31 for the 2026 tax year?**
No — unlike an IRA, 401(k) payroll contributions must happen through your paycheck before the calendar year ends. There's no grace period into the following year.

**Do I need to itemize to get a tax benefit from charitable giving this year?**
No. Starting in 2026, non-itemizers can deduct up to $1,000 ($2,000 married filing jointly) in cash gifts directly on Form 1040. Itemizers face a different rule — a new 0.5% AGI floor — so the right move depends on which way you file.

## The Bottom Line

The common thread across all five moves is the same: once the calendar turns to January, most of your 2026 tax options disappear. Harvest investment losses before the trade-date deadline (mindful of the 30-day wash sale window), capture any remaining 401(k) room from your last paychecks, lock in charitable gifts the IRS will actually recognize for 2026, use up "use it or lose it" FSA dollars, and sanity-check your bracket and withholding.
